Finnish keyword research: why translated keywords fail

By Tomas Pentikäinen, Finnish SEO strategist · 17 November 2026

Translated keyword lists fail in Finland for three reasons: Finnish changes the ending of a word depending on its role in the sentence, it joins words into long compounds, and the everyday word Finns type is often different from the dictionary translation. Finnish keyword research has to start from Finnish search data and group the variations under one page, instead of translating an English list line by line.

Here is what goes wrong, with real Finnish examples, and how I run keyword research for brands entering Finland.

One word, many forms

Finnish has 15 grammatical cases. Where English adds a preposition, Finnish changes the end of the word. Kenkä (shoe) shows up in searches as kengät, kenkiä, kengän and kengille, depending on what the searcher is asking.

Place names change too. "Dentist in Tampere" can be searched as hammaslääkäri tampere or hammaslääkäri tampereella, and Helsinki becomes Helsingissä. Google understands many of these forms as related, but keyword tools usually report each form as a separate keyword with its own small volume. If you only check the base form, you underestimate the demand and miss the form that actually ranks.

Compounds hide the real keyword

English writes "car insurance" as two words. Finnish writes autovakuutus. "Home insurance" is kotivakuutus, and "delivery costs" is toimituskulut.

This trips up translated lists in two ways. A translation tool may split the compound into two words, which looks wrong to a Finn and matches almost no searches. And a tool search for the head word vakuutus will not always surface the compounds that hold most of the demand. Each important compound needs its own check.

The dictionary word is often the wrong word

Dictionaries give the formal term, while people search with the word they say out loud. A mobile phone is matkapuhelin in the dictionary and puhelin or kännykkä in everyday speech. A laptop is kannettava tietokone on paper and kannettava or läppäri in conversation.

Which form wins depends on the category, the audience's age and the intent. Someone comparing models and someone looking for a repair shop may use different words for the same product. Only Finnish search data tells you which one to build the page around.

Small volumes need careful reading

Finland is a market of under six million people, so Finnish search volumes look small next to US numbers. In my experience, third-party tools also underestimate Finnish demand, sometimes heavily, and show zero for phrases that bring real traffic.

That changes how you read the data. Treat tool volumes as a ranking between keywords, not as a forecast. Cross-check with Google Search Console once the site has impressions, with Google Trends set to Finland, and with Google's own autocomplete in Finnish. A keyword with "10 searches a month" in a tool can still be the phrase that brings your first paying customers.

How I run Finnish keyword research

  1. Collect the topics from your English site and your sales team, as topics only, not as keywords.
  2. Find the Finnish phrases people use for each topic, including inflected forms, compounds and everyday synonyms.
  3. Check the live Finnish search results for each phrase to confirm the intent: are people looking to learn, compare or buy?
  4. Group the variations that share an intent under one page, so you do not create competing pages for kenkä and kengät.
  5. Map each group to a page, with the main phrase in the title, H1, URL and first paragraph.
  6. Review the plan against Search Console data a month after launch, and add the phrases you missed.

The same work feeds AI search. ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Google's AI Overviews answer Finnish questions from Finnish pages, and they quote the pages that answer clearly in the words people use.
